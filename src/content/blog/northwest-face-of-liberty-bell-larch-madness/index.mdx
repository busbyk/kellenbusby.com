You have to know what to do when things go wrong in climbing. So I think it's good to have things go just a little bit wrong so you can practice for when things go a lotta bit wrong.

Systems are a big part of why I like climbing: protecting a trad pitch well, managing ropes on a wall, finding my way through alpine terrain. It's about operating well in terrain that makes that difficult.

Sometimes things go really smoothly and you feel like you laced it. A totally pleasant day out, moving smoothly with your partner.

But sometimes you take the harder pitch because the rock looks better. And then the rock isn't really better. And then you bail, and the rope gets stuck. And then the easier pitch is full of hollow flakes. And you're tired. And it's cold, so Sean gets the screaming barfies.

But it's good to have this experience! It's practice in figuring it out, and it's nice to remember I still have all these skills. We even had some fun on this thing!

## The Morning

Well, we forgot about Larch Madness.

In our brief planning call of "hey, what do you want to climb?", neither of us came to the realization that it would be:

1. Quite cold on a Northwest-facing route in October and
2. Absolutely overrun by the PNW version of a leaf peeper (Larch Madness)

This made our not-so-early start not exactly the best move for the day (**mistake #1?**). Thankfully, our trail veered off from the Blue Lake trail and we still found solitude for most of the day. We just had to park pretty far down on the side of the highway and politely ask many a hiker if we could pass on the way. 😇

## The Climb

The first two pitches were chill and went smoothly. It was a little chilly. We were both climbing in puffies but it was only cold towards the end of the belay.

<BlogImage
  src={ropeUpCrackPitch}

It was a really, really cool perspective from this side of Liberty Bell. The view was amazing. We had a great view of [Golden Horn and Tower Mountain](https://linesclimbing.com/t/ffe66a5d-763d-4589-95da-be12bc78beed) which we'd scrambled on an overnight in fall 2024.

They're such dramatically shaped mountains. So cool.

After the top of the second pitch, you move your belay over 100ft and establish at the base of two different options. You can either take the original 5.8+ pitch on some very hollow-looking flakes or you can take a better-_looking_ pitch that starts with a 5.7 crack, steepens, then hits a .10d groove-to-slab section protected by two bolts.

Well, I thought I would just give the .10d a go because the first bit looked like better rock quality and "hey it's bolted, why not".

There turned out to be plenty of crumbly rock on this pitch too, including a big flake when you get into the steep bit that flexed when I hand jammed behind it. I promptly stopped jamming. And the first bolt looked a long way off given how steep the groove was 😅

I also thought that if I were to fall, it seemed likely that my feet would be landing pretty close to that flake that flexed.

I wanted to push myself to try this but I felt like there were enough reasons to back down:

- Fall potential was bigger than I'd hoped
- Falling would have landed me near some sketchy rock with my partner only slightly off to the side
- It was cold and we were in a position that would have been less than ideal to self-rescue from if I got hurt falling

Margins are smaller in alpine climbing. I like making safe decisions to climb (or ski or bike) another day.

It's unfortunate when those safe decisions result in your rope getting stuck though 😂

**Mistake #2** was taking this pitch. But **mistake #0**, which we didn't figure out until the raps, was taping the ends of my rope. I'd cut this rope from a longer rope and taped the ends. When I pulled it through two vertically offset cams, the taped end got caught in the lobes of the lower cam. Even with both of us yarding on it, we couldn't pull this thing.

So I had to re-climb the pitch, extend my upper piece, and lower off again, leaving two cams behind. Thankfully, the rope pulled without issue this time.

The 5.8+ pitch felt scary because I was already a little bit psyched out, but realistically, the gear wasn't _horrible_. The climbing was actually OK but I was pretty over it so I didn't enjoy the pitch very much.

Sean can't lead right now because he's coming back from knee surgery #2. So while I'm faffing around, Sean's just getting colder.

<BlogImage
  src={lookingDownAtClimbers}

Once he was finally able to climb after basically belaying me three times, his fingers and toes weren't having it. He had to stop mid-pitch and let them warm up and he got the screaming barfies! 😱 What, are we ice climbing?!

The pitch 3 belay was pretty directly above the gear I bailed off of so I fixed a line and rapped down on a gri gri to snag my gear. Sean had a nano traxion with him which made the jug back up much nicer than using my hollow block.

I had been bringing a tibloc with me on multi pitch climbs for a while but I stopped including it in my usual kit for some reason. And I always question whether I actually want to bring my gri gri on alpine climbs too, due to the weight. But I was happy to have both in this scenario and that might put any doubts I had about the gri gri to rest. And I just might start bringing a micro traxion most of the time too. They're both pretty nice to have.

<BlogImage

The "money pitch" was actually quite fun. Good movement. I was still just not fully _in it_ but got into a pretty good flow and enjoyed the interesting corner climbing the further along I got in the pitch.

The top was a little tricky to protect, as the MP description warns. But not too tricky and I got some great nut placements before it eased up.

Sean was psyched to find the sunshine.

Hot tip: the Mountain Project pitch description says go left on low to mid 5th class terrain from the 4th pitch belay. Do not do this.

Do like one 5th class move to the right and get on some 3rd class terrain and unrope shortly thereafter 😅

Sean led out left from the 4th pitch belay, thinking it would be chill and it was sunny and he was cold so I thought that would be great. I didn't think to check the topo to compare against the MP description 🤦‍♂️

Regrettably, this led to Sean having to downclimb back to the anchor due to some unprotected hard moves blocking the exit that way. This is exactly the situation his doctor told him not to get into so we're counting this as **mistake #3**.

## Wrap Up

It's good to know what to do when things go wrong and practice systems that let you do that. But even more important is being calm and able to work through problems efficiently with your climbing partner and not let it ruin your day. And I think we nailed that even thought we did get totally throttled on this route 😂
